# Planning method

The ideas behind the planner, and how each screen supports them.

## One guest list, many views

Everything starts from a single guest list with one entry per person. The RSVP lists, seating,
dashboard, gift tracking and budget are all views of that list, so you only ever update a guest in
one place.

- **Households** group the people invited together (a couple, a family). They are the unit for
  invitations, RSVPs and gift-giving. Seating doesn't force them together, because children often
  sit apart from their parents.
- **Categories** say whose guest someone is, or which circle they belong to: each partner's family,
  friends, work, community. Every guest has exactly one.
- **Groups** are labels that cut across categories (bridal party, travelling from overseas, needs
  accommodation, choir, dietary needs). A guest can be in any number of them.
- **Guest type** (adult, child, staff) sets the price per head.

## Plan first, then track replies

Before invitations go out, decide who is on the plan to reach your target headcount (your A-list).
Everyone else can be invited if space allows. As replies arrive, the **forecast** is everyone who has
said yes plus the planned guests who haven't replied yet. Before any replies it equals your plan, and
once everyone has replied it equals the actual attendance.

## Choose the headcount by cost, not just by size

Venues often charge less per head as the headcount grows, and bill a minimum number of guests. Guests
also bring gifts, which offset part of their cost. Taken together, a bigger guest list can cost less
overall.

**Worked example:** fewer than 180 guests at $179 per head, fewer than 220 at $157, 220 or more at
$139, and an average gift of $110 per guest.

| Guests | Venue cost | Expected gifts | Net cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| 180 | $28,260 | $19,800 | $8,460 |
| 220 | $30,580 | $24,200 | $6,380 |

Aiming for 220 costs less than 180. Just below a price tier it can even be cheaper to pay for the
tier's headcount and leave a few places empty: 175 guests on the 180 package (180 × $157 = $28,260)
cost less than 175 × $179 = $31,325. The headcount optimiser on the dashboard plots this for every
guest count and marks the cheapest.

## Compare venues like for like

A venue that includes drinks and catering extras looks more expensive than one with a large hire fee
and bring-your-own drinks, until you add those costs back. For each venue package, record:

- the hire fee, price per head and any per-head tiers, and the minimum and maximum guests
- discount, gratuity and tax
- whether drinks are included, a bar tab or bring-your-own, with an estimated cost per guest
- extras you'd have to buy separately (a DJ, the cake)
- what's included, side by side

The total at any headcount then compares like for like. Choosing a package can copy its pricing into
your planner settings.

## Budget from the top down

Start with what you can spend overall. Take off the non-negotiables (the venue at your forecast
headcount, and anything you mark as fixed), then share out the remainder by percentage. Moving one
share rebalances the others, and switching a category off shows what dropping it frees up. Estimates
and payments are tracked against each share.

## Seat by table themes

Give each table a theme ("Parents", "Young couples", "Work") and fill it from the attending guests.
Balance bars show the mix of each table by category, or by any attribute you choose to track (age
group, for example). Each table has its own capacity, so a roomier table is just one with fewer
guests.

## Gifts and thank-yous

Link each gift to the households it came from to see its value per attending guest, and compare that
with the average you assumed when choosing the headcount. A box plot shows the spread, including
households that gave nothing and the large outliers. An optional rule flags which gifts need a card,
for example gifts from people who couldn't attend, or gifts above a set amount per guest.
